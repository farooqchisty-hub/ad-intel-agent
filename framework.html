The Static Ad Framework

Derived from 153 competitor ads torn down structurally, checked against B2B creative research. What actually separates an ad that runs for a year from one killed in a fortnight.

22 September 2026 · for review

01The finding that overturns the previous approach

I compared the 49 ads in the corpus that ran 90 days or more against the 81 killed inside 30 days, on the structural measures the teardown captures. The result is consistent and it points the opposite way to how I was building.

Restraint outlives volume. Every instinct that says "make the hook enormous, make it shout" is measurably associated with ads that get killed. I had been enforcing a 30 percent hero carrier. The ads that actually survive sit at eleven percent.

The second finding is about proof. Among the fast-killed ads, 31 of 81 show their claim "as large type". Among survivors it is 16 of 49, and the rest show proof as an object: award badges, a document, a comparison panel, review cards, a rated strip. The category's durable ads do not shout the claim. They exhibit it.

02What the longest-running ads are actually built like

The nine ads at 729 days and five at 475 days, all Omnisend, share one construction almost exactly:

  • A saturated brand colour fills the entire frame. Not white, not cream. The colour is the canvas.
  • An oversized statement sits left, set directly in open space on that colour. Two or three lines. No box, no plate, no container.
  • A proof object sits right, inside a rounded container: three stacked award badges, three review cards, a comparison panel.
  • A single high-chroma accent carries either one line of the hook or the CTA. One colour doing one job.
  • The brand mark is small and in a corner. It is never the point.
  • Negative space runs 43 to 66 percent.

That is a two-column composition: say it left, prove it right. It is the single most replicated construction in the corpus and it has run for two years.

03What the research adds

The 2026 B2B creative sources converge with the corpus rather than contradicting it:

  • The claim sits in the top third; the proof or product occupies the lower half. Logo in a corner at modest scale. One CTA with nothing competing.
  • Legibility must land inside about 1.7 seconds, so the hierarchy is front-loaded: one thing clearly first, one clearly second, nothing else competing.
  • For a still, contrast is the motion. Luminance separation between subject and background is what triggers the eye in a feed.
  • Lean on one effect hard. Stacking curiosity, tension and humour in one frame reads as confusion.
  • Illustrated and designed visuals beat photographic stock in B2B. Stock lifestyle collapsed once the platforms began recognising library images.
  • Recurring B2B formats that work: the comparison grid, the three-stat scoreboard, the before and after split, the oversized single metric, the product screenshot with one specific real detail.

04The framework

Five layers. The first three are fixed by evidence and do not vary. The last two are where a style, an angle or a concept expresses itself. That split is what makes it durable: the thing that changes is not the thing that is load bearing.

Layer 1 · The canvas, fixed

  • One colour fills the frame. It comes from the brand base: forest, cream, charcoal or white.
  • Negative space at or above 45 percent. This is a target, not a leftover.
  • At most 5 colours in the whole image, and at most one secondary accent.
  • At most 4 distinct type sizes.

Layer 2 · The two-block grid, fixed

Every ad is a statement block and a proof block. Two focal points, which is what the corpus shows as normal, rather than the single focal point I was forcing.

  • Statement block: the hook, set directly in open space. No container, no highlight bar, no plate. Occupies roughly 10 to 12 percent of the frame. Upper-third or left, per the pattern chosen.
  • Proof block: an object that carries the evidence. Larger in area than the statement. This is the visual mass of the ad.

Layer 3 · Proof as an object, fixed

The claim is exhibited, not announced. The proof block is one of these, chosen by what the angle actually has to show:

Proof objectUse when the angle has
Comparison panel, two rows or columnsa structural difference, e.g. billed on sends against billed on contacts
Stacked cards, three of themseveral instances of one thing: reviews, integrations, channels
Document or artifactsomething that exists on paper: an invoice, a policy, a plan
Interface fragment, cropped confidentlythe product itself is the evidence
Single oversized figureone approved number that carries the whole argument

Layer 4 · Style, variable

The treatment of the two blocks. This is where an ad looks different from its neighbour without breaking the construction: flat graphic, illustrated, photographic, dimensional, editorial, or diagrammatic. Style changes the surface. It never moves the blocks.

Layer 5 · Concept, variable

What the proof object actually is for this hook. The same comparison panel can be a pricing table, a receipt, a docket or a scoreboard. The same stacked cards can be reviews, channels or plans. Concept is the invention layer, and it is bounded by the block it has to fill.

07Why this one should hold

  • The fixed layers are measured, not asserted. Every number in layers 1 to 3 comes from the survivor-versus-killed comparison, and it re-derives on every sweep.
  • The variable layers are genuinely free. Style and concept can go anywhere without touching the construction, which is what lets one framework serve many looks.
  • It fails loudly. Each fixed layer is a number a QA pass can check: negative space, colour count, type sizes, hook share, proof object present. Not taste.
  • It says no to the thing that keeps going wrong. The recurring failure has been over-production: too big, too loud, too many elements. Every fixed layer pushes the other way.
